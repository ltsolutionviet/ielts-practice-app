A second attempt at domesticating the tomato

A

It took at least 3,000 years for humans to learn how to domesticate the wild tomato and cultivate it for food. Now two separate teams in Brazil and China have done it all over again in less than three years. And they have done it better in some ways, as the re-domesticated tomatoes are more nutritious than the ones we eat at present. This approach relies on... CRISPR genome editing technique... ‘There are 50,000 edible plants in the world, but 90 percent of our energy comes from just 15 crops’ (Q23). ‘We can now mimic the known domestication course of major crops like rice, maize, sorghum or others (Q16),’ says Caixia Gao... ‘Then we might try to domesticate plants that have never been domesticated (Q16).’ (Q17)

B

Wild tomatoes... produce pea-sized fruits... peoples such as the Aztecs and Incas transformed the plant... resulting in desirable traits such as larger fruit. But every time a single plant with a mutation is taken from a larger population for breeding, much genetic diversity is lost (Q15). And sometimes the desirable mutations come with less desirable traits. For instance, the tomato strains grown for supermarkets have lost much of their flavour (Q24). By comparing the genomes of modern plants to those of their wild relatives... The teams in Brazil and China have now used this knowledge to reintroduce these changes from scratch while maintaining or even enhancing the desirable traits of wild strains.

C

Kudla’s team made six changes altogether. For instance, they tripled the size (Q25) of fruit by editing a gene called FRUIT WEIGHT... Newly domesticated one has five times as much lycopene. ‘They are quite tasty,’ says Kudla. ‘A little bit strong. And very aromatic’ (Q18). The team in China re-domesticated several strains of wild tomatoes with desirable traits lost in domesticated tomatoes (Q16). In this way they managed to create a strain resistant to a common disease called bacterial spot race (Q14), which can devastate yields. They also created another strain that is more salt (Q26) tolerant – and has higher levels of vitamin C.

D

Meanwhile, Joyce Van Eck... decided to use the same approach to domesticate the groundcherry (Q16)... But she adds that taking the work further would be expensive because of the need to pay for a licence for the CRISPR technology and get regulatory approval (Q22).

E

This approach could boost the use of many obscure plants, says Jonathan Jones... But it will be hard for new foods to grow so popular with farmers and consumers that they become new staple crops (Q20), he thinks. The three teams already have their eye on other plants that could be ‘catapulted into the mainstream’, including foxtail, oat-grass and cowpea (Q16). By choosing wild plants that are drought or heat tolerant, says Gao, we could create crops that will thrive even as the planet warms (Q19). But Kudla didn’t want to reveal which species were in his team’s sights, because CRISPR has made the process so easy (Q21).
