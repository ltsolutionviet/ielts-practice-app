Building the Skyline: The Birth and Growth of Manhattan’s Skyscrapers

Katharine L. Shester reviews a book by Jason Barr about the development of New York City

In Building the Skyline, Jason Barr takes the reader through a detailed history of New York City. The book combines geology, history, economics, and a lot of data to explain why business clusters developed where they did (Q27) and how the early decisions of workers and firms shaped the skyline we see today.

Barr begins chapter one by taking the reader on a ‘helicopter time-machine’ ride — giving a fascinating account of how the New York landscape in 1609 might have looked from the sky (Q32). He then moves on to a subterranean walking tour of the city, indicating the location of rock and water below the subsoil, before taking the reader back to the surface.

Chapters two and three take the reader up to the Civil War (1861–1865)... Both chapters are informative and well researched and set the stage for the economic analysis that comes later in the book (Q33). I would have liked Barr to expand upon his claim that existing tenements* prevented skyscrapers in certain neighborhoods because ‘likely no skyscraper developer was interested in performing the necessary “slum clearance”’... At first glance, it is not obvious why slum clearance would be limiting, while more expensive foundations would not (Q28).

Chapter four focuses on immigration and the location of neighborhoods... Most of these enclaves were located on the least valuable land, between the industries located on the waterfront and the wealthy neighborhoods bordering Central Park (Q35).

... Chapter six then presents data on building height throughout the 20th century... While less technical than the research paper on which the chapter is based, it is probably more technical than would be preferred by a general audience (Q29).

Chapter seven tackles the ‘bedrock myth’, the assumption that the absence of bedrock close to the surface between Downtown and Midtown New York (Q36) is the reason for skyscrapers not being built between the two urban centers. Rather, Barr argues that while deeper bedrock does increase foundation costs, these costs were neither prohibitively high (Q37) nor were they large compared to the overall cost of building a skyscraper (Q38). What I enjoyed the most about this chapter was Barr’s discussion of how foundations are actually built. He describes the use of caissons, which enable workers to dig down for considerable distances (Q39), often below the water table, until they reach bedrock. Barr’s thorough technological history discusses not only how caissons work, but also the dangers involved (Q40).

Chapters eight and nine focus on the birth of Midtown and the building boom of the 1920s. Chapter eight contains lengthy discussions of urban economic theory that may serve as a distraction to readers primarily interested in New York (Q30). However, they would be well-suited for undergraduates...

In the final chapter (chapter 10), Barr discusses another of his empirical papers that estimates Manhattan land values... The data work (Q31) that went into these estimations is particularly impressive (Q31).
