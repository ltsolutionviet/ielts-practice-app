Palm oil

A. Palm oil is an edible oil derived from the fruit of the African oil palm tree, and is currently the most consumed vegetable oil in the world. It’s almost certainly in the soap we wash with in the morning, the sandwich we have for lunch, and the biscuits we snack on during the day (Q16). Why is palm oil so attractive for manufacturers? Primarily because its unique properties – such as remaining solid at room temperature (Q23) – make it an ideal ingredient for long-term preservation, allowing many packaged foods on supermarket shelves to have ‘best before’ dates of months, even years, into the future.

B. Between 1990 and 2012, the global land area devoted to growing oil palm trees grew from 6 to 17 million hectares (Q18). From a mere two million tonnes of palm oil being produced annually globally 50 years ago, there are now around 60 million tonnes produced every single year (Q18), a figure looking likely to double or even triple by the middle of the century.

C. However, there are multiple reasons why conservationists cite the rapid spread of oil palm plantations as a major concern... Endangered species – most famously the Sumatran orangutan (Q20/24), but also rhinos, elephants, tigers, and numerous other fauna – have suffered from the unstoppable spread of oil palm plantations (Q20).

D. ‘Palm oil is surely one of the greatest threats to global biodiversity,’ declares Dr Farnon Ellwood...

E. One response to the boycott movement has been the argument for the vital role palm oil plays in lifting many millions of people in the developing world out of poverty (Q19). Is it desirable... given how many low-income people in developing countries depend on it for their livelihoods (Q19)?

F. Even the deforestation argument isn’t as straightforward as it seems. Oil palm plantations produce at least four and potentially up to ten times more oil per hectare than soybean, rapeseed, sunflower or other competing oils (Q14). ... Of course, if you’re cutting down virgin forest it’s terrible... But if it’s replacing rice, for example, it might actually sequester more carbon (Q14).

G. The industry is now regulated by a group called the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO) (Q15)... Over the past decade or so, an agreement has gradually been reached regarding standards (Q21/22:C) that producers of palm oil have to meet... The RSPO insists upon no virgin forest clearing, transparency (Q21/22:B) and regular assessment of carbon stocks (Q25), among other criteria.

H. New research at Ellwood’s lab hints at one plant which might make all the difference. The bird’s nest fern (Asplenium nidus) (Q17) grows on trees... Ellwood believes that reintroducing the bird’s nest fern into oil palm plantations could potentially allow these areas to recover their biodiversity (Q26), providing a home for all manner of species...
